If you run an engineering firm, there's a good chance your best work comes through word of mouth. Someone you did a decent job for mentions you to someone else, and the phone rings.

Nothing wrong with that. But there's a step in the middle that's easy to miss.

Between the recommendation and the phone call, the person who's been given your name goes and looks you up. Your engineering company website is where they land. The referral gets you a look. Whether it turns into an enquiry depends on what they find when they get there.

## What happens after someone recommends you

TREW Marketing and GlobalSpec survey engineers and technical buyers every year on how they research suppliers. Their 2025 report, with over a thousand respondents worldwide, found that technical buyers spend 60% of the buying process online on average. And 72% spend at least half of it online before choosing to speak to someone at the company.

Flip that round to your side of the desk. By the time they ring, they've usually done most of their homework.

The same research found 73% routinely turn to vendor websites and online technical publications. It's a global survey rather than a UK one, so don't hang too much on the exact numbers, but the direction's hard to argue with.

## Your website is the credibility check

This is the bit we'd underline. In the 2025 report, technical buyers were almost twice as likely to see a strong website as a sign of a company's credibility than a prominent sponsorship at a trade show.

TREW didn't dress it up either: "An informative and well-maintained website is the strongest indicator of quality for technical buyers assessing vendors."

Picture a firm that's spent good money on a stand, had a busy show, then sent every visitor back to a website nobody's touched in years. The stand got them interested. The website's where they decide if you're the real deal.

Engineers judge finish. A tired site gets judged the same way.

## What engineers actually want to find

Not a nicer banner. Asked which kinds of content they find most valuable, respondents in the 2025 report put datasheets top at 79%, then technical publication articles at 61%, CAD drawings at 37% and product demo videos at 35%.

Real information, in other words. Stuff they can use to check you can do the job before they've spoken to you.

For most engineering firms that comes down to a handful of things. Say what you make, straight away, so a stranger gets it within seconds of landing on the homepage. List your capability: machines, materials, sizes, tolerances, the processes you do in-house, whatever a buyer would ask on the first call. If you hold standards and approvals, put them where they can be found.

Then give them something technical worth saving, like a datasheet, a capability sheet or a drawing. Use photos of your own work, not stock shots of someone else's factory. And make the enquiry easy, with one clear route on every page that works on a phone.

Most of that is information you already hold. It just isn't on the site.

## The long quiet bit between orders

There's another reason the website matters, even if you're flat out right now.

Research by the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for the LinkedIn B2B Institute, reported by Marketing Week, found that up to 95% of businesses aren't in the market for most goods and services at any one time. Their reasoning is that companies change their providers of services around every five years, so only a small share are looking in any given quarter.

Two caveats, to be fair to it. Marketing Week says the 95% figure isn't a precise one for every sector. And the five-year example is about services like banking and software, not machined parts. Treat it as a rough guide.

Even so, most of your future customers aren't buying today. When they are, they tend to favour names they already know.

TREW's 2026 report points the same way. In eeNews Europe's write-up of it, 53% of technical buyers say brand familiarity influenced their most recent purchase, and 70% are more likely to choose a well-known brand when comparing similar solutions.

Word of mouth builds some of that familiarity. Your website, and whatever you put out between orders, has to do the rest.

## A quick check you can do this week

Before you spend a penny on marketing, give this half an hour.

1. Open your website on your phone, on mobile data. Does it load quickly? Can you read it without zooming?
2. Show the homepage to someone outside the business for five seconds. Ask them what you make.
3. Look for your machine list, materials and standards. Could a buyer find them in under a minute?
4. Is there anything technical a buyer could download and keep?
5. Send a test enquiry through your own site. Time the reply, if one comes at all.
6. Count how many of the photos are of your own work.

Pass all six? Good stuff. The referrals coming your way have somewhere decent to land.

Fail a couple and you've got a short list of jobs. None of them is a mystery.
